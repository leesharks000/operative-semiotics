Liberation philology: the non-indexed perfective and the alienation of 'for'

The Phase X papers of February 2026 (#417, #422, #432, #433) argue that English lacks a non-indexed perfective, tracing the aorist's loss through the Germanic drift, Latin administration, the Norman ledger and enclosure. The Missing Aspect (#434) specifies a diagnostic battery (translation variance, hedging, AI systems adding temporal indexing) not yet run, and forecasts that use seeds the form into the language. The Prepositional Alienation (#467, #892) argues that English 'for' fuses function with intent through Anglo-Norman and Latin calques and the 1540s 'for all intents and purposes', reads Thucydides' truest cause against stated reasons, proposes a dictionary of frictions, forecasts that the distinction will not develop naturally, and records the claim to be the founding document of liberation philology. Traversal logs (#473, #474) hold that AI summarizers' default intent attribution is this gap at work. The page also holds unrelated claims on narrative-field interventions, the James derivation, a forgery test and Wisse's translation.
